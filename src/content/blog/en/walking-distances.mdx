There is a particular kind of course that looks completely reasonable on paper
and rides badly. Every obstacle is the right height, the track flows, nothing is
in a corner. And then the first three horses all get to the double wrong, and by
the time you work out why, half the class has gone.

Almost always the problem is a distance nobody measured.

## The distance is between the middles, not between the poles

A one-stride double at "7.5 metres" means 7.5 metres from the centre of the
first obstacle to the centre of the second. If the first fence is a vertical and
the second is an oxer 1.4 m deep, and you measure from the back rail of the
vertical to the front rail of the oxer, you are about 70 cm short of where you
think you are. That is a third of a stride. It is the difference between a horse
meeting B comfortably and a horse chipping in.

This is why CourseForge measures obstacle to obstacle from their logical
centres, and why the app puts the width of every obstacle in metres in front of
you. An oxer is 1.4 m deep by default, a triple bar 1.8 m, open water 3.5 m.
Those numbers exist in the plan because they exist in the arena.

## Four distances worth getting right

**Combinations.** One stride is 7.0–7.9 m depending on what you want and who is
riding; two strides is 10.0–11.0 m. Pick the number deliberately and then build
to it with a tape, not with paces.

**Related distances on a straight line.** Anything up to about 25 m between two
fences on a straight line is a related distance whether you meant it to be one
or not. Horses will count it. If you have not decided how many strides it is,
the riders will decide for you and they will not all decide the same thing.

**The approach to water.** A Liverpool or open water after a long straight
arrives faster than the same fence off a turn. That is often the whole point,
but it should be a decision.

**The first fence.** Far enough from the start that a horse can be balanced and
in front of the leg, close enough that the clock is not doing the deciding.

## Strides are an estimate, and should be labelled as one

CourseForge will tell you a distance is about five strides. It says *about*
because a 4 m canter stride is an average, not a fact. A pony at 3.2 m and a
big-striding horse at 4.2 m will ride the same 20.5 m completely differently.

The number is there to stop you building something impossible. It is not there
to tell a rider how to ride it. Walking the course yourself is still the job.

## Build it, then walk it again

Measure on the plan, build to the measurement, then walk the actual line with
your own legs. Ground is not flat, footing is not even, and a fence that is
7.5 m from another fence on a screen can be 7.9 m in the arena because someone
dragged a stand while setting the wings.

The plan is how you think. The tape is how you check.
